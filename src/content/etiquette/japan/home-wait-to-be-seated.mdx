summary: Wait to be shown where to sit in a Japanese home — the far seat from the door (kamiza), especially one facing the tokonoma alcove, is the seat of honor, and claiming it yourself reads as presumptuous rather than confident.
context: >
  Japanese homes and rooms carry a built-in seating hierarchy: kamiza
  (上座), the seat farthest from the entrance, is reserved for the most
  honored person present, while shimoza (下座), nearest the door, is
  the humble seat, traditionally the host's own. A room with a tokonoma,
  a small decorative alcove for a scroll or flower arrangement, marks
  the seat facing it as the clearest kamiza of all. A guest choosing
  their own seat, especially the best one, skips past the host's role
  in assigning it and can come across as presumptuous rather than
  self-assured.

After the shoes-and-slippers routine at the entrance, stay standing near the door (the shimoza position) rather than walking further in and sitting down. Wait for your host to gesture toward a seat, or ask "Where should I sit?" if nothing's said.

If you're guided to the seat facing the tokonoma alcove or the one farthest from the door, that's the kamiza, the seat of honor, accept it rather than deflecting to the door-side seat out of modesty, insisting can be its own kind of awkward.

- **Don't treat the tokonoma alcove as a shelf or a place to set a bag** — it's display space for a scroll or flower arrangement, and its presence is what marks the seat in front of it as the room's most honored spot.
- **This hierarchy carries over into taxis, elevators, and meeting rooms too** — the same kamiza/shimoza logic decides who sits or stands where in a business setting, not just at someone's home.
