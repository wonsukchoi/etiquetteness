title: Never stand chopsticks upright in rice
country: japan
category: dining
severity: strict
summary: Don't stick your chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice, and don't pass food directly from your chopsticks to someone else's chopsticks.
context: >
  Both gestures echo funeral rites. Upright chopsticks in rice resemble
  incense sticks placed at a Buddhist altar, and chopstick-to-chopstick
  passing mirrors how cremated bones are passed between family members at
  a funeral, called kotsuage. Doing either at a meal genuinely unsettles
  Japanese guests; it's not just poor table manners, it reads as a bad
  omen or a mocking reference to death. They're the two most serious
  entries in a broader set of chopstick taboos (hashi tabu) that Japanese
  children are taught from an early age, alongside smaller manners like
  not rubbing chopsticks together or spearing food with them.

Rest your chopsticks flat across the top of your bowl or on a chopstick rest (hashioki) between bites, never planted upright in the rice.

If you want to share food, place it on the other person's plate with your chopsticks, or use the opposite (thicker) end of your own chopsticks to serve, don't hand it chopstick-tip to chopstick-tip.

A few more chopstick manners worth knowing, from the same family of taboos:

- **Don't rub disposable chopsticks together** to smooth splinters (*kosuribashi*) — it implies your host gave you cheap ones.
- **Don't spear food** with a chopstick to lift it (*tsukibashi*). If something won't cooperate, bring the bowl closer to your mouth instead.
- **Don't point** with your chopsticks, at a person, a dish, or anything else — treat them as tools for eating, not gesturing.
- **Don't cross them** on the table or rest; lay them parallel, tips pointing left.
