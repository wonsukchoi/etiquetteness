  Queueing is treated as a basic fairness norm, not just politeness:
  first come, first served, no exceptions for who seems in more of a
  hurry. The habit traces back to crowds at Industrial Revolution
  factory gates and became a full national reflex during WWII
  rationing, when queuing for scarce food and supplies was a daily
  fact of life, it's now considered central enough to British identity
  that it comes up in the UK's own citizenship test. Cutting a queue
  is one of the most reliably irritating things you can do to a
  British stranger, and people will say something if you do it, quietly
  or not.

Look for where the line actually starts before joining (in busy places it's not always obvious) and join at the back, not wherever looks convenient.

If you're unsure whether a queue has formed (a crowded bar, a busy counter), it's normal to ask "is this the queue?" or "who's next?" rather than assuming and pushing forward.

A queue forms almost anywhere a few people are waiting, not just formal lines: a bus stop, a coffee shop, a post office counter. If you have to step away briefly, saying "I'm just behind you" to the person nearby holds your spot without you needing to stand rigidly in place.
