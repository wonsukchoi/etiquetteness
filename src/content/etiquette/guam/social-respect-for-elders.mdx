summary: When greeting a Chamorro elder, take their offered right hand and give it a light sniff or kiss while saying "ñora" or "ñot" — the traditional respect gesture.
context: >
  Nginge' (literally "to sniff") is a living greeting gesture rooted in
  pre-colonial Chamorro belief that smell carries a person's essence. On
  meeting an elder (manåmko'), a younger person bows slightly and brings the
  back of the elder's raised right hand toward their nose for a light sniff,
  saying "ñora" (from Spanish señora) for a woman or "ñot" (señor) for a man.
  The elder may reply "Dioste ayudi" ("God help you"). In modern practice the
  gesture has evolved for many families into a light kiss on the hand or
  cheek instead of a sniff, but the meaning is the same. It's a concrete
  expression of mamåhlao, the broader Chamorro code of conduct that ties
  respect and humility to family honor — a common phrase parents use with
  children is "Mungga na un ma' mamåhlao" ("don't bring shame on us").

When you meet a Chamorro elder, offer a small bow and take their right hand (or lean in for a cheek), give it a light sniff or kiss, and say "ñora" or "ñot" as appropriate. Do this on arrival and again when you leave — it's a greeting and a farewell, not a one-time formality.

Beyond the gesture itself, let elders lead conversation, don't interrupt, and greet them before anyone else in the room. Family reputation is on the line here: a lapse in manners toward an elder reflects on your whole family, not just you — so when in doubt, err on the side of extra deference.
