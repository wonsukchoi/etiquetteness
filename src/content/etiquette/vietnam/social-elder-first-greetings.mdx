title: Greet the oldest person in the room first
country: vietnam
category: social
severity: casual
summary: When you walk into a Vietnamese home or meeting, greet the eldest person present before anyone else, and use a kinship term (older brother/sister, aunt/uncle) rather than a name when you address someone older than you.
context: >
  Vietnamese social interaction runs on Confucian-influenced respect for
  age and seniority, and that shows up directly in language: there's no
  neutral "you" the way English has one, so speakers pick a kinship
  term (anh for a man slightly older, chị for a woman slightly older,
  ông or bà for someone elderly) based on the other person's age
  relative to their own, every time they speak. Skipping the eldest
  person to greet whoever's nearest, or defaulting to a flat "you" and
  first name, reads as not having registered who's actually in the
  room, even when it isn't meant that way.
source: "Vietnamese Etiquette Guide – Viet Nam National Authority of Tourism"

Scan the room before you start greeting people, and go to whoever looks oldest first, even if they're seated furthest from the door or aren't the one who invited you. A right-hand handshake is fine for the actual greeting; the ordering is what carries the weight.

If you're learning any Vietnamese at all, learn the kinship terms before you learn much else — anh/chị for someone a bit older, ông/bà for someone elderly, em for someone younger than you. Getting the term roughly right matters more than getting the grammar around it right.

- **This is the same instinct behind the business-card handshake order** — greeting elders first at a meeting isn't a separate rule from the social one, it's the same hierarchy applied in a business setting.
