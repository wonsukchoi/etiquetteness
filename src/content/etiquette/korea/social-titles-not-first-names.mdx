title: Use a title or honorific, not a first name
country: korea

severity: strict
summary: Address anyone older or senior to you by their title, role, or an honorific suffix, not their first name, unless they've explicitly invited you to.
context: >

  naming system where using someone's bare first name signals either
  deep intimacy or a real breach of hierarchy, there's no neutral

  English. Titles (teacher, manager, older brother/sister — sunbae,
  hyung, unnie, depending on relationship) stand in for the name
  itself in daily address, even among people who know each other
  well. Skipping this with someone senior reads as presumptuous even
  when it's a genuine mistake rather than disrespect.

Default to a title, role, or relational term instead of a first name: teacher (seonsaengnim), a job title plus -nim, or an age/relationship term like sunbae (senior), hyung/oppa/unnie/noona depending on your and their gender and relative age. In formal or business settings, last name plus title is the safe default.

Wait for the other person to explicitly invite first-name use before switching to it, even if you're on friendly terms — they'll usually say so directly ("just call me [name]") when they're comfortable with it.

- **This applies even to people just slightly older than you** — a one-year age gap in Korea is enough to set the senior-junior dynamic and the naming convention that comes with it.
- **Peers your own age or younger can go by first name freely** — the caution is specifically about anyone senior in age or rank, not a blanket rule for every interaction.
